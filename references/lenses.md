# Conversion lenses

Rate each lens **Strong**, **Okay**, **Weak**, **Missing**, or **Can't tell**, with one line of evidence from the actual page. Each lens below gives the question it answers, what to check, and the common ways it fails. Weigh lenses by context: for a $10 impulse buy, risk reversal barely matters; for a $3,000 service, it can decide everything.

## 1. Clarity
**Question:** Within five seconds, can a stranger say what this is, who it's for, and what they get?

Check:
- Does the headline state a concrete outcome or product, or is it a slogan ("Reimagine your workflow")?
- Does the subheadline explain how, for whom, or what it is?
- Would someone who arrived from an ad or search with no context understand it?
- Is jargon explained, or does it assume the visitor already knows the category?

Common failures: clever or abstract headlines; leading with the company's mission instead of the buyer's outcome; describing features with no picture of the result; the product's actual form (app, service, physical item, course) never stated.

## 2. Relevance and message match
**Question:** Does the visitor immediately feel "this is for me," and does the page deliver what got them to click?

Check:
- Is the target customer named or clearly implied?
- Does the page speak to the visitor's situation, or only describe the product?
- If traffic comes from ads, search, or social posts, does the first screen match that promise?
- Is the page trying to serve so many audiences that it speaks to none?

Common failures: generic copy that could fit any competitor; an audience too broad ("for everyone"); a mismatch between the ad's hook and the page's headline.

## 3. Value and differentiation
**Question:** Why this instead of the alternatives, including doing nothing?

Check:
- Is there a specific reason to choose this over the obvious alternatives (from step 3 of the skill)?
- Is the differentiator something buyers actually care about, or something only the owner finds impressive?
- Are benefits stated as outcomes ("finish your taxes in 20 minutes"), not just features ("AI-powered")?
- Does the page address free or cheaper options head-on when they exist?

Common failures: listing features any competitor also has; claiming "best" or "easiest" with nothing to back it up; ignoring the free option every buyer knows about; the real differentiator buried mid-page.

## 4. Proof
**Question:** What evidence shows this actually works for people like me?

Check:
- Testimonials: are they specific (results, numbers, before and after), attributed (name, photo, role, location), and relevant to the target buyer?
- Numbers: users, orders, results, ratings, years in business.
- Case studies, reviews with star ratings, press or awards, recognizable customer logos.
- Demonstrations: real screenshots, videos, product photos in use, sample output.
- Is proof placed near decision points (hero, pricing, call to action), or only in one section?

Common failures: no proof at all; vague praise ("Great product!"); anonymous quotes; screenshots labeled "Example" with no real results; proof that's all in one strip that buyers scroll past.

## 5. Trust and credibility
**Question:** Is this a real, legitimate business I can safely give money and information to?

Check:
- Who is behind it: founder or team, about page, location, contact options.
- Professional polish: typos, broken images, placeholder text, outdated copyright years.
- Domain and brand signals (unusual domains can raise doubts, especially for older or more cautious buyers).
- Security and payment signals at checkout, recognized payment methods.
- Privacy and data handling, especially for sensitive data or minors.
- Policies: terms, privacy, refund, shipping — present and easy to find.

Common failures: no human faces or names anywhere; no way to contact anyone; missing policies; a look that feels template-made or unfinished.

## 6. Risk reversal
**Question:** What happens if it doesn't work out for me?

Check:
- Free trial, free tier, or sample; and whether a card is required.
- Money-back guarantee: does it exist, how long, how easy, and is it visible near the price?
- Easy cancellation stated plainly.
- Free returns or exchanges (physical products).
- Outcome guarantees where appropriate.

Common failures: a guarantee that exists but only in the footer or terms; no guarantee on a considered or high-ticket purchase; cancellation that sounds hard or isn't mentioned; trial terms that are unclear.

## 7. Pricing and value framing
**Question:** Can buyers easily find the price, understand it, and feel it's worth it?

Check:
- Is the price visible without hunting or signing up? (Hidden prices are often a deal-breaker outside enterprise sales.)
- Are plans easy to compare? Is one plan recommended?
- Is the price anchored against something (a tutor's hourly rate, a competitor, the cost of the problem)?
- Is the cost reframed in buyer-friendly terms (per day, per use, total cost) where it helps — without becoming confusing?
- Are there surprise costs: shipping, setup fees, renewal terms?
- Do headline price claims match the actual plans?

Common failures: too many plans; confusing per-unit math; prices that don't match between sections; no anchor, so the price feels arbitrary; shipping revealed only at checkout.

## 8. Offer and call to action
**Question:** Is it obvious what to do next, and is the ask the right size for this moment?

Check:
- Is there one primary action, clearly stated, and repeated at natural decision points?
- Does the button label say what happens next ("Start free trial," "Add to cart — $29") rather than something vague ("Submit," "Learn more")?
- Is the ask proportionate? (Asking for a call or a credit card before giving enough value or proof is a common leak.)
- Do different buttons that look the same lead to different things, or different-looking buttons lead to the same thing?
- Is the offer itself compelling (bonuses, bundles, what exactly is included)?

Common failures: competing calls to action of equal weight; the same generic label on every button, including paid plans; a high-commitment ask too early; no call to action in the first screen.

## 9. Friction and flow
**Question:** How much effort stands between wanting it and having it?

Check:
- Steps and form fields required to sign up or buy; forced account creation.
- Whether signup or checkout pages (view only) restate value and reassurance or feel cold and demanding.
- Page length and structure relative to the decision (long is fine for high-ticket if it's organized; painful for impulse buys).
- Slow-loading or broken elements, if observable.
- Mobile considerations, if you can tell.

Common failures: long forms asking for data that isn't needed yet; checkout that drops all reassurance; navigation that leads buyers away from the purchase path.

## 10. Objection handling
**Question:** Does the page answer the doubts buyers actually have, where they have them?

Check:
- Is there an FAQ, and does it answer real buyer objections (price, comparison, fit, time, results, cancellation) or only operational questions?
- Are the biggest doubts addressed near where they arise (price doubts by the price, quality doubts by the product)?
- Does the page acknowledge the obvious "why not just use X" question?

Common failures: FAQs that answer questions no one asked; the biggest objection never mentioned; reassurance placed far from the moment of doubt.

## 11. Urgency and motivation
**Question:** Why act now instead of later?

Check:
- Is there a genuine reason to act now: a deadline, a seasonal need, a limited offer, a cost of waiting?
- Is the problem's pain made vivid enough that delay feels costly?
- Is any urgency honest? (Fake countdown timers and false scarcity erode trust and should be flagged, not recommended.)

Common failures: nothing to beat "I'll do it later"; urgency that feels manufactured; a real deadline (exam date, season, event) that the page never uses.

## 12. Visual hierarchy and readability
**Question:** Does the design guide the eye to what matters, in the right order?

Check (often needs screenshots; mark "Can't tell" from text alone):
- What the eye lands on first, and whether that's the most important message.
- Contrast and prominence of the primary button.
- Scannability: headings, short paragraphs, whitespace.
- Images that show the product or outcome rather than generic stock.
- Consistency between sections; nothing that looks broken or unfinished.

Common failures: walls of text; low-contrast buttons; decorative images that say nothing; the most important proof or offer visually buried.
